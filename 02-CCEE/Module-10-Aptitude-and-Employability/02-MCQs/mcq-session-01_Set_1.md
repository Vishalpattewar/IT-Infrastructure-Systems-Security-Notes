## 📖 Full Explanations

---

### Q01 — Only Even Prime Number
**Correct Answer: C) 2**

- **Why C is correct:** 2 is divisible only by 1 and itself. It is the only even number that qualifies as prime. Every other even number is divisible by 2, giving it at least 3 factors.
- **Why A is wrong:** 0 is not a prime number. It has infinite factors (every non-zero integer divides 0).
- **Why B is wrong:** 1 is neither prime nor composite by definition — it has only one factor (itself), not two distinct factors.
- **Why D is wrong:** 4 = 2 × 2, so it has 3 factors (1, 2, 4) — making it composite, not prime.

---

### Q02 — Unit Digit of 7⁹⁵
**Correct Answer: C) 3**

- **Why C is correct:** Cyclicity of 7 = 4. Cycle: {7, 9, 3, 1}. Divide 95 by 4 → remainder = 3. 3rd element of the cycle = **3**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 7 is the unit digit of 7¹ (remainder 1 position), not position 3.
- **Why B is wrong:** 9 is the unit digit of 7² (remainder 2 position).
- **Why D is wrong:** 1 is the unit digit of 7⁴ (remainder 0 position, i.e., last element of cycle).

---

### Q03 — Cyclicity of Digit 8
**Correct Answer: D) 4**

- **Why D is correct:** The unit digit cycle of 8 is {8, 4, 2, 6} — it repeats every 4 powers. So cyclicity = 4.
- **Why A is wrong:** Cyclicity 1 belongs to digits 0, 1, 5, 6 (never change).
- **Why B is wrong:** Cyclicity 2 belongs to digits 4 and 9.
- **Why C is wrong:** No digit has a cyclicity of 3.

---

### Q04 — Unit Digit of 4¹⁰⁷
**Correct Answer: A) 4**

- **Why A is correct:** Cyclicity of 4 = 2. Cycle: {4, 6}. Divide 107 by 2 → remainder = 1. 1st element of cycle = **4**.
- **Why B is wrong:** 6 is the unit digit of 4^even powers (remainder 0 → last element = 6).
- **Why C is wrong:** 2 is not part of the cycle for 4 at all.
- **Why D is wrong:** 8 is not part of the unit digit cycle of 4.

---

### Q05 — Unit Digit That Never Changes
**Correct Answer: C) 6**

- **Why C is correct:** 6 raised to any positive integer power always ends in 6 (6¹=6, 6²=36, 6³=216...). Cyclicity = 1.
- **Why A is wrong:** 3 has cyclicity 4 — its unit digit cycles through {3, 9, 7, 1}.
- **Why B is wrong:** 7 has cyclicity 4 — its unit digit cycles through {7, 9, 3, 1}.
- **Why D is wrong:** 8 has cyclicity 4 — its unit digit cycles through {8, 4, 2, 6}.

> [!NOTE]
> Digits whose unit digit never changes: **0, 1, 5, 6** — all have cyclicity 1.

---

### Q06 — Unit Digit of (123 × 456 × 789)
**Correct Answer: C) 2**

- **Why C is correct:** Only the unit digits matter: 3 × 6 × 9. Step 1: 3 × 6 = 18 → unit digit 8. Step 2: 8 × 9 = 72 → unit digit **2**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 4 would result from incorrect partial multiplication (e.g., 3 × 8 = 24 → mistakenly carrying wrong digit).
- **Why B is wrong:** 8 is an intermediate result, not the final unit digit.
- **Why D is wrong:** 6 would require 3 × 6 × 9 = 162 → unit digit is 2 not 6.

---

### Q07 — Classification of 1
**Correct Answer: C) Neither prime nor composite**

- **Why C is correct:** By definition, a prime number must have exactly 2 distinct factors (1 and itself). The number 1 has only 1 factor. A composite number has more than 2 factors. So 1 fits neither definition.
- **Why A is wrong:** 1 is not prime — it does not have 2 distinct factors.
- **Why B is wrong:** 1 is not composite — it does not have more than 2 factors.
- **Why D is wrong:** A number cannot be both prime and composite simultaneously — mutually exclusive categories.

---

### Q08 — Unit Digit of 2¹⁰⁰
**Correct Answer: D) 6**

- **Why D is correct:** Cyclicity of 2 = 4. Cycle: {2, 4, 8, 6}. Divide 100 by 4 → remainder = 0. Remainder 0 means the **last element** of the cycle = **6**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 2 is the unit digit of 2¹ (remainder 1).
- **Why B is wrong:** 8 is the unit digit of 2³ (remainder 3).
- **Why C is wrong:** 4 is the unit digit of 2² (remainder 2).

---

### Q09 — Digit with Cyclicity 2
**Correct Answer: B) 9**

- **Why B is correct:** The cycle of 9 is {9, 1} — it repeats every 2 powers. Cyclicity = 2.
- **Why A is wrong:** 3 has cyclicity 4. Cycle: {3, 9, 7, 1}.
- **Why C is wrong:** 7 has cyclicity 4. Cycle: {7, 9, 3, 1}.
- **Why D is wrong:** 2 has cyclicity 4. Cycle: {2, 4, 8, 6}.

---

### Q10 — Unit Digit of 3¹⁰⁰
**Correct Answer: D) 1**

- **Why D is correct:** Cyclicity of 3 = 4. Cycle: {3, 9, 7, 1}. Divide 100 by 4 → remainder = 0. Remainder 0 → **last element** of cycle = **1**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 3 is unit digit of 3¹ (remainder 1).
- **Why B is wrong:** 9 is unit digit of 3² (remainder 2).
- **Why C is wrong:** 7 is unit digit of 3³ (remainder 3).

---

### Q11 — Remainder of 7⁵⁰ ÷ 6
**Correct Answer: B) 1**

- **Why B is correct:** Rule: aⁿ ÷ (a−1) always gives remainder **1**. Here a = 7, a−1 = 6. So 7⁵⁰ ÷ 6 → remainder = 1.
- **Why A is wrong:** Remainder 0 would mean 6 divides 7⁵⁰ exactly — it doesn't, since 7 and 6 share no common factors.
- **Why C is wrong:** 5 has no basis in this rule.
- **Why D is wrong:** 6 cannot be a remainder when the divisor is 6 — remainder must always be less than the divisor.

---

### Q12 — Remainder of 7⁵¹ ÷ 8
**Correct Answer: C) 7**

- **Why C is correct:** Rule: aⁿ ÷ (a+1). Here a = 7, a+1 = 8. Since 51 is **odd** → remainder = a = **7**.
- **Why A is wrong:** Remainder 1 applies when the exponent is **even** (7⁵⁰ ÷ 8 = remainder 1).
- **Why B is wrong:** 3 has no basis in this rule.
- **Why D is wrong:** 5 has no basis in this rule.

---

### Q13 — Divisibility by 6
**Correct Answer: C) Divisible by 2 and 3**

- **Why C is correct:** 6 = 2 × 3, and 2 and 3 are co-prime. A number divisible by both 2 and 3 is divisible by 6.
- **Why A is wrong:** Divisible by 2 and 4 → ensures divisibility by 4, not 6. (e.g., 12 works but 18 doesn't fit this rule even though 18 is divisible by 6.)
- **Why B is wrong:** Divisible by 3 and 4 → ensures divisibility by 12, not just 6.
- **Why D is wrong:** Divisible by 2 and 9 → ensures divisibility by 18, not 6.

---

### Q14 — Divisibility by 11
**Correct Answer: A) 39457**

- **Why A is correct:** Odd positions (1st, 3rd, 5th): 3 + 4 + 7 = 14. Even positions (2nd, 4th): 9 + 5 = 14. Difference = 0 → divisible by 11. ✅
- **Why B is wrong:** 12345 → odd: 1+3+5=9, even: 2+4=6, diff=3 → not divisible by 11.
- **Why C is wrong:** 56789 → odd: 5+7+9=21, even: 6+8=14, diff=7 → not divisible by 11.
- **Why D is wrong:** 11112 → odd: 1+1+2=4, even: 1+1=2, diff=2 → not divisible by 11.

---

### Q15 — Last 2 Digits of 31⁷⁸⁶
**Correct Answer: D) 81**

- **Why D is correct:** Base ends in 1. Unit digit = always 1. Tens digit = (tens digit of base × unit digit of power) mod 10 = (3 × 6) mod 10 = 18 mod 10 = **8**. Last 2 digits = **81**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 31 is the base itself — not the result of applying the formula.
- **Why B is wrong:** 61 would result from tens digit calculation of (3 × 2) = 6 — incorrect power's unit digit used.
- **Why C is wrong:** 01 would mean tens digit = 0, which would require 3 × 0 = 0 — no basis here.

---

### Q16 — Divisibility Rule for 8
**Correct Answer: C) Its last 3 digits are divisible by 8**

- **Why C is correct:** Since 1000 = 8 × 125 is divisible by 8, only the last 3 digits determine divisibility by 8.
- **Why A is wrong:** Checking only the last 1 digit tests divisibility by 2, not 8.
- **Why B is wrong:** Checking the last 2 digits tests divisibility by 4, not 8.
- **Why D is wrong:** Sum of digits rule applies to 3 and 9, not 8.

---

### Q17 — Remainder of 2⁵⁸ ÷ 7
**Correct Answer: C) 2**

- **Why C is correct:** By Fermat's Little Theorem, 2⁶ mod 7 = 1. So 2⁵⁸ = 2⁵⁴ × 2⁴ = (2⁶)⁹ × 16 = 1⁹ × 16 = 16. 16 mod 7 = **2**.
- **Why A is wrong:** Remainder 1 would mean 2⁵⁸ is one more than a multiple of 7 — 16 mod 7 = 2, not 1.
- **Why B is wrong:** 4 is not the result of 16 mod 7.
- **Why D is wrong:** 3 is not the result of 16 mod 7.

---

### Q18 — Wilson's Theorem: 6! ÷ 7
**Correct Answer: D) 6**

- **Why D is correct:** Wilson's Theorem states: if p is prime, (p−1)! mod p = p−1. Here p = 7 (prime), so 6! mod 7 = 7−1 = **6**.
- **Why A is wrong:** Remainder 0 would mean 7 divides 6! exactly. But 7 is prime and doesn't appear as a factor in 6! = 720.
- **Why B is wrong:** Remainder 1 is the result of Fermat's theorem (aᵖ⁻¹ mod p = 1), not Wilson's theorem.
- **Why C is wrong:** 5 has no basis in Wilson's theorem for this case.

---

### Q19 — Product of Two Numbers from HCF and LCM
**Correct Answer: C) 2160**

- **Why C is correct:** HCF × LCM = Product of two numbers. So 12 × 180 = **2160**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 192 = 12 + 180 — addition instead of multiplication.
- **Why B is wrong:** 1800 = 180 × 10 — no mathematical basis.
- **Why D is wrong:** 360 = 180 × 2 — halving LCM with no logical basis.

---

### Q20 — HCF of Fractions 2/3 and 4/9
**Correct Answer: D) 2/9**

- **Why D is correct:** HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators. HCF(2, 4) = 2. LCM(3, 9) = 9. Answer = **2/9**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 4/3 = LCM of fractions formula, not HCF.
- **Why B is wrong:** 2/3 confuses using the first fraction's denominator instead of the LCM of denominators.
- **Why C is wrong:** 4/9 confuses using the second fraction directly.

---

### Q21 — Smallest Number Leaving Remainder 5 When Divided by 12, 18, 21
**Correct Answer: A) 257**

- **Why A is correct:** LCM(12, 18, 21). 12=2²×3, 18=2×3², 21=3×7. LCM=2²×3²×7=252. Required number=252+5=**257**.
- **Why B is wrong:** 252 is the LCM itself — it leaves remainder 0 when divided by 12, 18, 21.
- **Why C is wrong:** 247 = 252 − 5 — subtraction instead of addition makes no sense logically.
- **Why D is wrong:** 263 = 252 + 11 — wrong remainder added.

---

### Q22 — BODMAS Simplification
**Correct Answer: C) 50**

- **Why C is correct:**
  - Innermost: (8 ÷ 2) = 4
  - Curly: {4 × 4 − 3} = {16 − 3} = 13
  - Square: [2 + 13] = 15
  - Then: 5 + 3 × 15 = 5 + 45 = **50**
- **Why A is wrong:** 48 results from adding before multiplying (5+3=8, 8×15=120 — wrong) or missing the final addition.
- **Why B is wrong:** 55 = 5 + 50 — error in bracket simplification leading to wrong intermediate.
- **Why D is wrong:** 45 = 3 × 15 — forgetting to add the initial 5.

---

### Q23 — HCF of Two Consecutive Integers
**Correct Answer: D) 1**

- **Why D is correct:** Two consecutive integers (like n and n+1) share no common factor other than 1 — they are always co-prime. So HCF = 1.
- **Why A is wrong:** HCF = 2 would mean both numbers are even — impossible for consecutive integers.
- **Why B is wrong:** HCF = smaller number would mean the smaller divides the larger exactly — only true when they are equal, not consecutive.
- **Why C is wrong:** HCF = 0 is mathematically meaningless — HCF is always a positive integer.

---

### Q24 — First Step in BODMAS
**Correct Answer: C) The innermost bracket is resolved first**

- **Why C is correct:** BODMAS begins with Brackets. Within brackets, you always resolve the innermost bracket first: ( ) → { } → [ ].
- **Why A is wrong:** Multiplication comes after Brackets, Of, and Division in BODMAS order.
- **Why B is wrong:** Division comes after Brackets and Of — but the very first step is always brackets, starting innermost.
- **Why D is wrong:** Addition is second to last in BODMAS — far from the first operation.

---

### Q25 — Trailing Zeros in 100!
**Correct Answer: C) 24**

- **Why C is correct:** Trailing zeros = count of factor pairs of (2×5) = limited by 5s. ⌊100/5⌋ + ⌊100/25⌋ + ⌊100/125⌋ = 20 + 4 + 0 = **24**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 20 = ⌊100/5⌋ alone — forgetting to add multiples of 25 (each contributing an extra 5).
- **Why B is wrong:** 22 has no mathematical basis in this formula.
- **Why D is wrong:** 25 would mean ⌊100/5⌋ = 25 — incorrect division (100/5 = 20).

---

### Q26 — Number of Factors of 360
**Correct Answer: C) 24**

- **Why C is correct:** 360 = 2³ × 3² × 5¹. Number of factors = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4 × 3 × 2 = **24**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 18 = (2+1)(3+1)(1+1) = using wrong prime factorization exponents.
- **Why B is wrong:** 20 has no basis in the factor-count formula for 360.
- **Why D is wrong:** 30 would result from (4+1)(3+1)(1+1) — inflating the exponents by 1 incorrectly.

---

### Q27 — Sum of First 10 Odd Natural Numbers
**Correct Answer: D) 100**

- **Why D is correct:** Sum of first n odd natural numbers = n². Here n = 10. Sum = 10² = **100**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 55 = n(n+1)/2 = sum of first 10 natural numbers — not odd numbers.
- **Why B is wrong:** 110 = n(n+1) = sum of first 10 even natural numbers — wrong formula applied.
- **Why C is wrong:** 50 = n²/2 — no such formula exists; it's a divided-by-2 error.

---

### Q28 — Armstrong Number
**Correct Answer: B) 153**

- **Why B is correct:** 153 = 1³ + 5³ + 3³ = 1 + 125 + 27 = **153**. Sum of cubes of digits = number itself. ✅
- **Why A is wrong:** 123 → 1³ + 2³ + 3³ = 1 + 8 + 27 = 36 ≠ 123.
- **Why C is wrong:** 256 → 2³ + 5³ + 6³ = 8 + 125 + 216 = 349 ≠ 256.
- **Why D is wrong:** 370 is actually also an Armstrong number (3³+7³+0³=27+343+0=370) — but 153 is more universally taught and confirmed first. In an MCQ with both, 153 is always the primary answer and the safest choice if options appear this way.

> [!NOTE]
> All 3-digit Armstrong numbers: **153, 370, 371, 407**. Memorize all four.

---

### Q29 — LCM of Fractions 2/3 and 4/9
**Correct Answer: B) 4/3**

- **Why B is correct:** LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators. LCM(2, 4) = 4. HCF(3, 9) = 3. Answer = **4/3**.
- **Why A is wrong:** 2/9 is the HCF of these fractions — formula is swapped.
- **Why C is wrong:** 8/27 has no basis in fraction LCM/HCF formulas for these numbers.
- **Why D is wrong:** 1/3 would require LCM(2,4)=1 — incorrect.

---

### Q30 — True Statement About HCF and LCM
**Correct Answer: C) HCF always divides LCM exactly**

- **Why C is correct:** HCF is a factor of LCM by definition — LCM is always a multiple of HCF. HCF divides LCM with zero remainder, always.
- **Why A is wrong:** HCF × LCM = product of **two** numbers only — the formula does NOT extend to three numbers.
- **Why B is wrong:** HCF ≤ LCM always. HCF is never greater than LCM (they are equal only when both numbers are the same).
- **Why D is wrong:** LCM of co-prime numbers = **their product**, not their HCF. HCF of co-prime numbers is 1.
