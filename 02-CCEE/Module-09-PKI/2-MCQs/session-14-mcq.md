**Q1. The Information Technology Act 2000 is
India's primary digital law. On which date
did it come into FORCE?**

- A) June 9, 2000 — the date it was enacted
     by Parliament
- B) October 17, 2000 — the date it was
     officially notified into force ✅
- C) January 1, 2001 — the beginning of the
     new millennium
- D) February 5, 2009 — the date the 2008
     Amendment came into force

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — October 17, 2000:** The IT Act 2000
>   has two important dates:
>   **June 9, 2000** — enacted (passed by
>   Parliament and received Presidential assent).
>   **October 17, 2000** — came into force
>   (officially notified and became operational).
>   MCQ trap: both dates are related to IT Act
>   2000 — the question asks when it came INTO
>   FORCE, which is October 17, 2000.
> - ❌ **A:** June 9, 2000 is the date the IT Act
>   was **enacted** (passed) — not when it came
>   into force. There is a gap between these two
>   dates — a common exam trap.
> - ❌ **C:** January 1, 2001 has no significance
>   for the IT Act. This is a fabricated date
>   that sounds plausible because of the Y2K /
>   millennium context surrounding IT in 2000.
> - ❌ **D:** February 5, 2009 is when the
>   **IT (Amendment) Act 2008** came into force
>   — not the original IT Act 2000. This option
>   traps students who confuse the original act
>   with its amendment.

**Q2. The Information Technology Act 2000 was
based on a model law developed by which
international body?**

- A) ISO (International Organization for
     Standardization)
- B) ITU-T (International Telecommunication
     Union)
- C) UNCITRAL (United Nations Commission on
     International Trade Law) — Model Law on
     Electronic Commerce 1996 ✅
- D) IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force)

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — UNCITRAL 1996:** The IT Act 2000
>   is based on the **UNCITRAL Model Law on
>   Electronic Commerce (1996)** — a model
>   framework developed by the United Nations
>   Commission on International Trade Law to
>   harmonize electronic commerce laws globally.
>   India adapted this framework for its domestic
>   legislation.
> - ❌ **A:** ISO develops standards for
>   products and processes (e.g., ISO 27001
>   for information security management) —
>   it did not develop the model law that the
>   IT Act is based on.
> - ❌ **B:** ITU-T develops telecommunications
>   standards (X.509, X.800) — not commercial
>   law frameworks. ITU-T standards are
>   technical, not legal model laws.
> - ❌ **D:** IETF develops internet technical
>   standards (RFCs) like TLS, HTTP, SMTP —
>   not commercial or legal model laws.

**Q3. Under the IT Act 2000, which section
establishes the Controller of Certifying
Authorities (CCA)?**

- A) Section 3
- B) Section 5
- C) Section 17 ✅
- D) Section 43

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — Section 17:** The CCA (Controller
>   of Certifying Authorities) is established
>   under **Section 17** of the IT Act 2000.
>   The CCA is the apex regulatory body for
>   PKI in India — licensing Certifying
>   Authorities, operating the RCAI (Root
>   Certifying Authority of India), and
>   overseeing DSC issuance.
> - ❌ **A — Section 3:** Section 3 deals with
>   the **authentication of electronic records**
>   using digital signatures — it defines how
>   digital signatures must work technically,
>   not who regulates them.
> - ❌ **B — Section 5:** Section 5 provides the
>   **legal recognition of digital signatures**
>   — stating they are legally equivalent to
>   handwritten signatures. It is the legal
>   validity provision, not the regulatory
>   body establishment provision.
> - ❌ **D — Section 43:** Section 43 deals with
>   **penalties for unauthorized access** to
>   computer systems — a cybercrime provision
>   in Chapter VII. Nothing to do with the CCA.

**Q4. Section 5 of the IT Act 2000 states
that a digital signature satisfies any
legal requirement for a signature. What is
this called?**

- A) Digital authentication equivalence
- B) Electronic signature mandate
- C) Legal recognition of digital signatures ✅
- D) Cryptographic validity presumption

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — Legal recognition:** Section 5
>   provides the **legal recognition of digital
>   signatures** — it states that wherever any
>   law requires a handwritten signature, a
>   digital signature satisfying the requirements
>   of Section 3 shall be deemed to satisfy
>   that requirement. This makes digital
>   signatures legally equivalent to handwritten
>   signatures under Indian law.
> - ❌ **A:** "Digital authentication equivalence"
>   is not the formal term used in the IT Act.
>   The IT Act uses the phrase "legal recognition
>   of digital signatures" — Section 5 is
>   the specific provision.
> - ❌ **B:** "Electronic signature mandate"
>   implies a requirement — Section 5 is about
>   permissive legal validity, not mandating use.
>   It says digital signatures ARE legally valid,
>   not that they MUST be used.
> - ❌ **D:** "Cryptographic validity presumption"
>   relates more to Section 85B (presumption of
>   secure electronic records) — not Section 5.
>   Section 5 is specifically about legal
>   recognition, not cryptographic presumptions.

**Q5. Under the IT Act 2000, which offence
carries the MOST SEVERE punishment — up to
life imprisonment?**

- A) Section 66 — Computer hacking
- B) Section 66C — Identity theft
- C) Section 67 — Publishing obscene content
- D) Section 66F — Cyber terrorism ✅

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **D — Section 66F — Cyber terrorism:**
>   Section 66F covers attacks on critical
>   infrastructure, computer networks of
>   government or public services, or any
>   act intended to threaten national unity
>   or cause mass harm. The punishment is
>   **up to LIFE IMPRISONMENT** — the most
>   severe under the IT Act.
> - ❌ **A — Section 66 (Hacking):** Section
>   66 covers computer-related offences
>   (hacking, data destruction) — punishment
>   is up to **3 years imprisonment OR fine
>   up to ₹5 lakh**. Significant but far
>   below life imprisonment.
> - ❌ **B — Section 66C (Identity theft):**
>   Identity theft using electronic means —
>   punishment is up to **3 years imprisonment
>   AND fine up to ₹1 lakh**. More limited than
>   Section 66F.
> - ❌ **C — Section 67 (Obscene material):**
>   Publishing obscene material electronically —
>   punishment is up to **3 years and ₹5 lakh
>   fine** for first offence. Not the most
>   severe under IT Act.

**Q6. Section 43A was added by the IT
(Amendment) Act 2008. What does it cover?**

- A) Punishment for hacking government
     computer systems
- B) Compensation payable by body corporates
     for failure to protect sensitive
     personal data ✅
- C) Rules for Certifying Authorities to
     issue digital signature certificates
- D) Offences related to electronic signature
     forgery

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Section 43A — Corporate data
>   protection liability:** Section 43A makes
>   body corporates (companies, organizations)
>   liable to pay compensation if they fail to
>   implement reasonable security practices
>   and sensitive personal data is compromised
>   as a result. Unlike Section 43 (which has
>   a ₹1 crore cap), Section 43A has no fixed
>   upper limit on compensation. It is India's
>   closest equivalent to GDPR's corporate
>   accountability provisions.
> - ❌ **A:** Hacking government systems falls
>   under **Section 66** (general hacking) and
>   **Section 70** (protected systems). Section
>   43A is specifically about DATA PROTECTION
>   failure by corporate bodies — not hacking.
> - ❌ **C:** Rules for Certifying Authorities
>   are in **Sections 35–42** of Chapter VI.
>   Section 43A is in Chapter VII (Penalties)
>   — entirely different chapter and purpose.
> - ❌ **D:** Electronic signature forgery
>   relates to **Section 84B** — punishment for
>   publishing false digital signature
>   certificates. Section 43A is about data
>   protection failure, not signature forgery.

**Q7. The IT Act 2000 does NOT apply to
certain types of documents. Which of the
following CANNOT use electronic or digital
signatures under the IT Act?**

- A) GST returns filed with the government
- B) Income Tax Returns (ITR) submitted
     electronically
- C) A sale deed for the transfer of
     immovable property ✅
- D) A loan agreement signed between a
     bank and a borrower

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — Property sale deed:** The **First
>   Schedule to the IT Act** lists documents
>   excluded from electronic signature validity.
>   **Sale of immovable property** (land, real
>   estate) is explicitly excluded — sale deeds
>   must be executed on stamp paper with physical
>   signatures and registered under the
>   Registration Act 1908. e-Sign and DSC
>   cannot substitute for this.
> - ❌ **A:** GST returns are filed electronically
>   using digital signatures (DSC) or Aadhaar-
>   based e-Sign — this is one of the most
>   common uses of digital signatures in India.
>   GST filings are fully covered by IT Act.
> - ❌ **B:** Income Tax Returns are filed
>   electronically and signed using Aadhaar
>   OTP-based e-Sign or DSC — a primary
>   use case for e-Sign. ITR filings are
>   fully covered by IT Act.
> - ❌ **D:** Loan agreements between banks
>   and borrowers are routinely executed using
>   e-Sign on mobile applications — fully
>   valid under the IT Act. Property sale
>   deeds are the specific exclusion, not
>   loan agreements.

**Q8. What is the Root Certifying Authority
of India (RCAI) and who operates it?**

- A) RCAI is India's highest court for
     digital evidence disputes — operated
     by the Supreme Court
- B) RCAI is India's national Root CA —
     operated by the CCA (Controller of
     Certifying Authorities) ✅
- C) RCAI is a private company that issues
     the highest-assurance DSCs in India
- D) RCAI is the IETF working group that
     defines India's PKI standards

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — National Root CA operated by CCA:**
>   The **Root Certifying Authority of India
>   (RCAI)** is India's national Root CA —
>   the trust anchor for all PKI in India.
>   It is **operated by the CCA** (Controller
>   of Certifying Authorities) under MeitY.
>   The RCAI signs the certificates of all
>   licensed Certifying Authorities in India
>   (eMudhra, (n)Code, SafeScrypt, etc.) —
>   establishing the chain of trust.
> - ❌ **A:** RCAI is a Certificate Authority —
>   not a court or dispute resolution body.
>   Digital evidence disputes are handled by
>   courts under the Indian Evidence Act and
>   IT Act provisions — not by RCAI.
> - ❌ **C:** RCAI is a government-operated
>   Root CA — not a private company. Private
>   licensed CAs (eMudhra, etc.) issue the
>   actual DSCs to subscribers. RCAI sits
>   above them as the trust anchor.
> - ❌ **D:** RCAI has nothing to do with
>   IETF — which is an international body
>   developing internet protocols. RCAI is
>   India's domestic Root CA operating under
>   the IT Act.

## MCQs 9–14 — LDAP and Active Directory

**Q9. What does LDAP stand for, and which
RFC defines its current version?**

- A) Lightweight Data Access Protocol —
     RFC 2251
- B) Lightweight Directory Access Protocol
     — RFC 4511 ✅
- C) Linked Directory Authentication
     Protocol — RFC 3748
- D) Local Directory and Access Protocol —
     RFC 5246

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Lightweight Directory Access
>   Protocol, RFC 4511:** LDAP stands for
>   **Lightweight Directory Access Protocol**.
>   The current version **LDAPv3** is defined
>   in **RFC 4511** (published 2006). LDAPv3
>   is the version universally implemented in
>   all modern directory services including
>   Active Directory, OpenLDAP, and others.
> - ❌ **A:** "Lightweight Data Access Protocol"
>   is wrong — it is "Directory" not "Data."
>   RFC 2251 was the original LDAPv3 RFC
>   (1997) — it was later obsoleted and
>   replaced by RFC 4511 in 2006. The current
>   RFC is 4511.
> - ❌ **C:** "Linked Directory Authentication
>   Protocol" is fabricated. RFC 3748 defines
>   **EAP (Extensible Authentication Protocol)**
>   — completely unrelated to LDAP.
> - ❌ **D:** "Local Directory and Access Protocol"
>   is fabricated. RFC 5246 defines **TLS 1.2**
>   — also completely unrelated to LDAP.

---

**Q10. LDAP uses two different port numbers
depending on whether the connection is
encrypted. What are the correct ports?**

- A) Port 80 for plain LDAP — Port 443
     for LDAPS
- B) Port 389 for plain LDAP — Port 636
     for LDAPS ✅
- C) Port 143 for plain LDAP — Port 993
     for LDAPS
- D) Port 389 for plain LDAP — Port 389
     for LDAPS (same port, different setting)

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — 389 / 636:** LDAP uses two
>   well-known ports:
>   **Port 389** — plain LDAP (unencrypted
>   or with STARTTLS upgrade).
>   **Port 636** — LDAPS (LDAP over TLS —
>   encrypted from the start).
>   Using port 389 without TLS is dangerous
>   because the BIND operation sends credentials
>   in cleartext.
> - ❌ **A:** Port 80 and 443 are for HTTP and
>   HTTPS respectively — not LDAP. These are
>   web protocol ports, not directory service
>   ports.
> - ❌ **C:** Port 143 and 993 are for IMAP and
>   IMAPS (Internet Message Access Protocol)
>   — email retrieval ports. Not LDAP.
> - ❌ **D:** LDAP and LDAPS use DIFFERENT port
>   numbers — 389 and 636. Using the same port
>   for both would make it impossible to
>   distinguish between encrypted and
>   unencrypted connections at the network
>   level.

**Q11. In LDAP, what is a Distinguished Name
(DN) and what does the following example
represent?**

`cn=Alice Smith,ou=Users,dc=example,dc=com`

- A) A search filter that finds all users
     named Alice Smith in the example.com
     domain
- B) The full unique path to Alice Smith's
     entry in the LDAP directory tree ✅
- C) An encrypted identifier that hides
     Alice's real identity in the directory
- D) A group membership claim that places
     Alice in the Users and example.com
     organizational units

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Full unique path (DN):** A
>   **Distinguished Name (DN)** is the full,
>   unique identifier for an entry in the LDAP
>   directory tree — like a complete address.
>   Reading left (most specific) to right
>   (least specific):
>   `cn=Alice Smith` → the specific person,
>   `ou=Users` → in the Users organizational unit,
>   `dc=example,dc=com` → in the example.com
>   domain. Together this uniquely identifies
>   exactly one entry in the entire directory.
> - ❌ **A:** LDAP search filters use a different
>   syntax — e.g., `(cn=Alice Smith)` in
>   parentheses. A DN is not a search filter —
>   it is an address/locator, not a query.
> - ❌ **C:** Distinguished Names are NOT
>   encrypted — they are plaintext identifiers
>   visible to anyone who queries the directory.
>   The DN tells the directory WHERE an entry
>   is — not a hidden identity.
> - ❌ **D:** A DN is an address — not a group
>   membership claim. Group memberships are
>   stored as attributes (like `memberOf`) in
>   the LDAP entry — separately from the DN.

**Q12. What LDAP operation is used to
authenticate a user to the directory server?**

- A) SEARCH — queries the directory for
     matching entries
- B) MODIFY — updates user attributes
- C) BIND — authenticates the user to the
     LDAP server ✅
- D) COMPARE — verifies if an attribute
     matches a value

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — BIND:** The **BIND operation** is
>   the LDAP authentication mechanism. A
>   Simple BIND sends the user's Distinguished
>   Name (DN) and password to the server. The
>   server verifies the credentials and returns
>   success or failure. This MUST be protected
>   by LDAPS (port 636) or STARTTLS — otherwise
>   the DN and password are sent in cleartext.
> - ❌ **A:** SEARCH queries the directory for
>   entries matching a filter — it is used to
>   look up users, groups, computers etc.
>   SEARCH can be performed anonymously (without
>   authentication in many directories) — it
>   is not the authentication operation.
> - ❌ **B:** MODIFY updates attributes of an
>   existing entry (e.g., changing a phone
>   number or adding a group member). It
>   requires authentication BEFORE being
>   performed — but MODIFY itself is not the
>   authentication operation.
> - ❌ **D:** COMPARE tests whether a specific
>   attribute has a specific value and returns
>   TRUE or FALSE — useful for password
>   verification in some implementations, but
>   BIND is the standard authentication
>   operation for establishing an authenticated
>   session.

**Q13. What is Active Directory (AD) and
what is its relationship to LDAP?**

- A) Active Directory is an alternative to
     LDAP — organizations choose one or the
     other for their directory needs
- B) Active Directory is Microsoft's
     enterprise directory service that
     implements LDAP as its access protocol,
     adding Kerberos, DNS, and Group Policy ✅
- C) Active Directory is a hardware appliance
     that stores LDAP data on dedicated
     network-attached storage
- D) Active Directory is IETF's reference
     implementation of the LDAPv3 standard

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — AD implements LDAP plus more:**
>   **Active Directory** is Microsoft's
>   enterprise directory service (Windows
>   Server). It **implements LDAP** as its
>   primary access protocol — applications
>   query AD using LDAP. On top of LDAP, AD
>   adds: **Kerberos** (authentication),
>   **DNS** (name resolution), **Group Policy**
>   (centralized security config), and **ADCS**
>   (built-in PKI). AD is far more than just
>   LDAP — but LDAP is the protocol used to
>   access it.
> - ❌ **A:** AD and LDAP are not alternatives —
>   AD USES LDAP. You do not "choose between"
>   them — if you use Active Directory, you
>   can query it with LDAP. OpenLDAP is an
>   alternative to AD's directory functionality,
>   but not to LDAP the protocol.
> - ❌ **C:** Active Directory is a software
>   service (Windows Server role — AD DS) —
>   not a hardware appliance. It runs on
>   Windows Server domain controllers. NAS
>   devices are storage appliances — unrelated.
> - ❌ **D:** IETF maintains the LDAPv3 standard
>   through RFC documents (RFC 4511) — it does
>   not create reference implementations.
>   Active Directory is Microsoft's proprietary
>   implementation — not an IETF project.

**Q14. Active Directory Certificate Services
(ADCS) is integrated with Active Directory.
What is one of its most useful features for
enterprise certificate management?**

- A) ADCS automatically purchases certificates
     from commercial CAs on behalf of the
     organization
- B) ADCS auto-enrolls certificates to
     domain-joined computers and users via
     Group Policy — without manual requests ✅
- C) ADCS replaces Active Directory — it is
     the newer version of the directory service
- D) ADCS only issues certificates to
     external users — internal users use
     Kerberos tickets instead

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Auto-enrollment via GPO:** One of
>   ADCS's most powerful features is **auto-
>   enrollment** — using Group Policy, ADCS
>   can automatically issue certificates to
>   all domain-joined computers and users.
>   When a computer starts or a user logs in,
>   it automatically requests and receives
>   the appropriate certificate from ADCS —
>   zero administrator or user interaction.
>   This is how enterprises deploy machine
>   certificates for EAP-TLS Wi-Fi at scale.
> - ❌ **A:** ADCS is an INTERNAL CA — it issues
>   its OWN certificates. It does not purchase
>   certificates from external commercial CAs.
>   External CA certificates (DigiCert, Sectigo)
>   are used for public-facing services — ADCS
>   is for internal PKI.
> - ❌ **C:** ADCS is a separate Windows Server
>   role (Certification Authority) that runs
>   alongside AD — it does not replace AD.
>   Both work together. AD handles
>   authentication and directory; ADCS handles
>   certificate issuance within the AD framework.
> - ❌ **D:** ADCS issues certificates to
>   INTERNAL users and computers — that is its
>   primary purpose (internal PKI). External
>   users would typically be issued certificates
>   by commercial CAs. Kerberos tickets and
>   certificates serve different purposes and
>   both are used in enterprise environments.

**Q15. Blockchain was first described in a
whitepaper published in 2008. Who published
this whitepaper and what was the title?**

- A) Vitalik Buterin — "Ethereum: A Next
     Generation Smart Contract Platform"
- B) Satoshi Nakamoto — "Bitcoin: A
     Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System" ✅
- C) Nick Szabo — "Smart Contracts:
     Building Blocks for Digital Markets"
- D) Wei Dai — "b-money: An Anonymous,
     Distributed Electronic Cash System"

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Satoshi Nakamoto, Bitcoin
>   whitepaper:** The blockchain concept was
>   introduced in the **Bitcoin whitepaper**
>   titled **"Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic
>   Cash System"** published by the pseudonymous
>   **Satoshi Nakamoto** in **October 2008**.
>   The Bitcoin network went live on
>   January 3, 2009. Satoshi Nakamoto's true
>   identity remains unknown.
> - ❌ **A:** Vitalik Buterin published the
>   Ethereum whitepaper in **2013** (not 2008)
>   — Ethereum was launched in 2015. Buterin
>   extended Bitcoin's blockchain concept to
>   include Turing-complete smart contracts.
> - ❌ **C:** Nick Szabo coined the concept of
>   "smart contracts" in **1994** and proposed
>   "Bit Gold" in 1998 — precursors to Bitcoin.
>   But he did not publish the 2008 blockchain/
>   Bitcoin whitepaper.
> - ❌ **D:** Wei Dai published the "b-money"
>   proposal in **1998** — an early digital
>   cash concept that influenced Bitcoin.
>   Satoshi Nakamoto referenced b-money in
>   the Bitcoin whitepaper — but Dai did not
>   create blockchain.

**Q16. What is the fundamental problem that
blockchain was designed to solve without
requiring a trusted central authority?**

- A) The encryption problem — existing
     algorithms were too slow for digital
     payments
- B) The double-spend problem — preventing
     the same digital currency from being
     spent twice without a central ledger ✅
- C) The identity problem — verifying user
     identities without government-issued IDs
- D) The storage problem — distributing
     large files across the internet without
     central servers

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — The double-spend problem:**
>   Digital money can be copied — Alice could
>   send the same $100 digital token to both
>   Bob AND Carol simultaneously. Traditional
>   solution: trust a bank to maintain a single
>   ledger and prevent double-spending.
>   Blockchain's innovation: replace
>   institutional trust with distributed
>   cryptographic consensus — every node
>   maintains a copy of the ledger, and
>   majority consensus prevents double-spending
>   without any central authority.
> - ❌ **A:** Encryption speed was not the
>   primary problem blockchain solved. Bitcoin
>   uses existing cryptographic algorithms
>   (SHA-256, ECDSA) — it did not need to
>   invent new algorithms. The problem was
>   distributed trust, not encryption speed.
> - ❌ **C:** Identity verification is addressed
>   by PKI, biometrics, and government systems —
>   not blockchain's primary purpose. Blockchain
>   uses pseudonymous addresses (derived from
>   public keys) — it actually provides LESS
>   identity verification than traditional
>   systems.
> - ❌ **D:** Distributed file storage is
>   addressed by systems like IPFS, Bittorrent,
>   and CDNs — not blockchain. Blockchain is
>   a distributed ledger for transaction records,
>   not a general file distribution system
>   (though IPFS integrates with some blockchains).

**Q17. A blockchain block contains a field
called "Previous Block Hash." What is the
security purpose of this field?**

- A) It provides the encryption key for
     decrypting the current block's transactions
- B) It cryptographically links blocks
     together — changing any block changes
     its hash, breaking the link to all
     subsequent blocks ✅
- C) It identifies which miner created the
     previous block so rewards can be
     distributed
- D) It stores a backup copy of the previous
     block in case of network failures

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Cryptographic chaining:**
>   The Previous Block Hash field creates
>   the "chain" in blockchain. Each block
>   contains the SHA-256 hash of the previous
>   block's header. If an attacker modifies
>   Block 5 (e.g., changes a transaction):
>   → Block 5's hash changes
>   → Block 6's "Previous Block Hash" no
>   longer matches → Block 6 is invalid
>   → All subsequent blocks are invalid
>   → Attacker must recompute ALL blocks —
>   computationally infeasible in large networks.
>   This is blockchain's core tamper-resistance.
> - ❌ **A:** Blocks are NOT encrypted — the
>   Previous Block Hash is not an encryption
>   key. Blockchain transaction data is
>   typically public and visible to all
>   participants. Blockchain provides integrity
>   and transparency — not confidentiality.
> - ❌ **C:** The Previous Block Hash is a
>   cryptographic hash of the block header —
>   it does not identify miners. Miner identity
>   and rewards are tracked separately through
>   the coinbase transaction in each block.
> - ❌ **D:** The Previous Block Hash is a
>   32-byte hash value — not a backup copy of
>   the previous block. Storing full block
>   backups in each block would be extremely
>   inefficient and is not how blockchain works.

**Q18. What is Proof of Work (PoW) in
blockchain, and which major cryptocurrency
uses it?**

- A) A method where validators vote on
     valid blocks — used by Ethereum
- B) A consensus mechanism where miners
     compete to solve a SHA-256 cryptographic
     puzzle — used by Bitcoin ✅
- C) A process where new users prove they
     have worked in the blockchain industry
     before participating
- D) A digital signature scheme where
     block creators prove they own their
     private key

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — SHA-256 mining puzzle, Bitcoin:**
>   In Proof of Work, miners compete to find
>   a **nonce** value such that
>   SHA-256(SHA-256(block_header)) produces
>   a hash below a target value (starts with
>   enough zeros). This requires massive
>   computation (quadrillions of attempts).
>   The first miner to find the valid nonce
>   gets to add the block and receive the
>   block reward. **Bitcoin** uses PoW.
>   Its primary disadvantage is enormous
>   energy consumption.
> - ❌ **A:** Validators voting on valid blocks
>   describes **Proof of Stake (PoS)** — used
>   by Ethereum (since September 2022 "The
>   Merge"). PoS replaced Ethereum's original
>   PoW. PoW is specifically the mining puzzle
>   mechanism.
> - ❌ **C:** "Proof of work experience in
>   blockchain" is a humorous misreading —
>   PoW is a cryptographic puzzle-solving
>   mechanism, not employment verification.
>   The "work" refers to computational work
>   (hashing).
> - ❌ **D:** Proving ownership of a private
>   key is a digital signature operation —
>   not PoW. PoW is about computational
>   difficulty (solving hash puzzles), not
>   cryptographic key ownership proofs.

**Q19. What is the "Genesis Block" in a
blockchain?**

- A) The most recently added block —
     because it is where new transactions
     start
- B) A special administrative block that
     stores the list of all authorized miners
- C) Block 0 — the very first block in the
     blockchain, with no previous block hash ✅
- D) The block containing the highest-value
     transactions in the blockchain's history

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — Block 0, the first block:**
>   The **Genesis Block** is block number 0 —
>   the very first block in any blockchain.
>   It has no predecessor, so its "Previous
>   Block Hash" field is set to all zeros.
>   Bitcoin's Genesis Block was mined by
>   Satoshi Nakamoto on **January 3, 2009**.
>   Every subsequent block traces back to
>   the Genesis Block through the chain.
> - ❌ **A:** The most recently added block
>   is simply called the "latest block" or
>   the "block at the chain tip" — it has
>   no special name. The Genesis Block is
>   specifically the FIRST block — not the
>   most recent.
> - ❌ **B:** There is no administrative block
>   storing authorized miners — Bitcoin is
>   a permissionless network where anyone
>   can mine. The Genesis Block has no
>   special administrative function beyond
>   being the starting point of the chain.
> - ❌ **D:** Transaction value does not
>   determine block significance in
>   blockchain. The Genesis Block happened
>   to contain a single coinbase transaction
>   (50 BTC reward to Satoshi) — it is
>   special because it is FIRST, not because
>   of transaction value.

**Q20. What is a Merkle Root in the context
of a blockchain block, and what is its
purpose?**

- A) The private key of the miner who
     created the block — used to sign the
     block header
- B) The hash of all the block's transactions
     organized in a Merkle tree — enables
     efficient transaction verification ✅
- C) The network address of the blockchain
     node that validated the block
- D) The cryptographic seed used to generate
     the block's random nonce

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Hash of all transactions via
>   Merkle tree:** The **Merkle Root** is a
>   single hash value that represents ALL
>   transactions in a block — computed using
>   a Merkle tree structure (binary tree where
>   leaf nodes = transaction hashes, parent
>   nodes = hash of children). The Merkle Root
>   is stored in the block header. Its key
>   benefit: a **Merkle proof** allows verifying
>   a single transaction's inclusion in a block
>   with only O(log n) hashes — without
>   downloading all transactions.
> - ❌ **A:** Private keys and signing operations
>   are related to transaction signing and miner
>   identity — not the Merkle Root. The Merkle
>   Root is a hash of transaction data —
>   no private key is involved in its creation.
> - ❌ **C:** Node network addresses are not
>   stored in the Merkle Root or block header.
>   Block propagation and node addresses are
>   handled at the P2P network layer — not
>   encoded in the Merkle Root.
> - ❌ **D:** The nonce is a separate field in
>   the block header that miners modify to
>   solve the PoW puzzle. The Merkle Root is
>   computed from transaction data — it is
>   not related to nonce generation.

**Q21. Ethereum switched from Proof of Work
to Proof of Stake. What was this event called
and when did it happen?**

- A) The Fork — January 2021
- B) The Merge — September 2022 ✅
- C) The Upgrade — December 2020
- D) The Transition — July 2015

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — The Merge, September 2022:**
>   Ethereum's transition from PoW (energy-
>   intensive mining) to PoS (validators stake
>   ETH as collateral) was called **"The Merge"**
>   and occurred in **September 2022**. It
>   merged Ethereum's original execution layer
>   with a new PoS consensus layer (Beacon
>   Chain). Energy consumption dropped by
>   approximately 99.95% after the Merge.
> - ❌ **A:** "The Fork" in January 2021 is not
>   a significant named Ethereum event. Hard
>   forks in Ethereum are named after cities
>   (London, Berlin, etc.). The PoW-to-PoS
>   transition is specifically called "The
>   Merge" and occurred in September 2022.
> - ❌ **C:** December 2020 was when the
>   Ethereum Beacon Chain (the PoS consensus
>   layer) launched — but the actual transition
>   of the main Ethereum network happened
>   in September 2022 with "The Merge."
> - ❌ **D:** July 2015 was when **Ethereum
>   mainnet launched** — the initial public
>   release of Ethereum using Proof of Work.
>   Not the PoS transition.

**Q22. What is a Smart Contract in the
context of Ethereum?**

- A) A legally binding digital agreement
     signed using an S/MIME certificate
- B) A self-executing program stored on
     the blockchain whose terms are written
     in code — executes automatically when
     conditions are met ✅
- C) A type of digital signature that
     automatically expires after one use —
     similar to e-Sign
- D) A cryptographic token that represents
     ownership of a physical contract
     document

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Self-executing blockchain program:**
>   A **smart contract** is a program deployed
>   on the Ethereum blockchain (written in
>   Solidity). It executes automatically when
>   predetermined conditions in its code are
>   met — no human intermediary needed. Example:
>   "If Alice sends 1 ETH to this contract
>   address by date X, transfer ownership of
>   digital asset Y to Alice." Once deployed,
>   the code is immutable and transparent.
> - ❌ **A:** A legally binding digital agreement
>   signed with S/MIME describes **email-based
>   digital signing** under IT Act provisions —
>   not a blockchain smart contract. Smart
>   contracts may have legal implications but
>   their defining feature is automated code
>   execution — not signature format.
> - ❌ **C:** A single-use digital signature
>   describes **e-Sign** (Aadhaar-based
>   one-time certificate) — not a smart contract.
>   Smart contracts are persistent programs —
>   not one-time signing mechanisms.
> - ❌ **D:** NFTs (Non-Fungible Tokens) represent
>   digital ownership — but a smart contract
>   is the underlying program, not the token
>   itself. Smart contracts can CREATE NFTs —
>   but they are programs, not tokens.

**Q23. The 51% attack is a known blockchain
vulnerability. What does it allow an attacker
to do?**

- A) Decrypt all transactions on the
     blockchain by controlling the
     majority of private keys
- B) Control more than 50% of the network's
     hash power — allowing rewriting of
     recent transaction history and
     double-spending ✅
- C) Permanently delete 51% of all
     historical blocks from the blockchain
- D) Steal coins from 51% of wallet
     addresses simultaneously using
     brute force

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **B — Rewrite history and double-spend:**
>   In a Proof of Work blockchain, if an
>   attacker controls **more than 50% of total
>   network hash power**, they can:
>   (1) Build an alternative version of the
>   blockchain faster than the honest network.
>   (2) Replace recent blocks with their own
>   version — reversing transactions.
>   (3) Double-spend: spend coins, then erase
>   the transaction from their controlled chain.
>   This is "51% attack" — it breaks the
>   fundamental immutability guarantee.
> - ❌ **A:** Transactions on most public
>   blockchains are not encrypted — they are
>   publicly visible. Private keys are not
>   stored on the blockchain and cannot be
>   accessed by controlling hash power. The
>   51% attack is about consensus control,
>   not cryptographic key access.
> - ❌ **C:** Blockchain is designed to be
>   **append-only** — blocks cannot be
>   permanently deleted even with 51% hash
>   power. A 51% attacker can rewrite RECENT
>   history (replace the last few blocks) but
>   cannot erase the deep historical chain —
>   the computational work required grows
>   exponentially for older blocks.
> - ❌ **D:** Brute-forcing private keys from
>   wallet addresses is a completely separate
>   attack — it requires breaking ECDSA
>   cryptography (computationally infeasible
>   for secp256k1 with current technology).
>   A 51% attack is about consensus control,
>   not key cryptanalysis.

**Q24. Bitcoin uses SHA-256 for its Proof
of Work. What hashing algorithm does
Ethereum use?**

- A) SHA-256 — same as Bitcoin
- B) SHA-3 (standard NIST version)
- C) MD5 — for faster block processing
- D) Keccak-256 — a SHA-3 family variant ✅

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **D — Keccak-256:** Ethereum uses
>   **Keccak-256** for hashing — a member
>   of the SHA-3 family but NOT the same as
>   the final NIST SHA-3 standard (FIPS 202).
>   Keccak was the SHA-3 competition winner
>   but Ethereum implemented it before NIST
>   finalized the standard (NIST made slight
>   modifications). Keccak-256 is used for
>   computing Ethereum addresses, transaction
>   hashes, and block hashes.
> - ❌ **A:** Bitcoin uses SHA-256 (double —
>   SHA256(SHA256(data))). Ethereum uses
>   Keccak-256 — a different algorithm.
>   This is a specific distinction that exam
>   questions test — Bitcoin = SHA-256;
>   Ethereum = Keccak-256.
> - ❌ **B:** The standard NIST SHA-3 (FIPS 202)
>   has slight padding differences from the
>   original Keccak submission. Ethereum uses
>   the original **Keccak-256** (pre-NIST
>   modification) — not the finalized NIST
>   SHA-3. These produce different outputs
>   for the same input.
> - ❌ **C:** MD5 is broken and produces only
>   128-bit output — completely unsuitable
>   for a cryptocurrency. Ethereum would
>   never use MD5 — which has known collision
>   attacks since 2004.

**Q25. Which type of blockchain uses a
group of organizations as validators —
semi-decentralized — and is commonly
used in financial consortia?**

- A) Public blockchain — open to anyone,
     fully decentralized (e.g., Bitcoin)
- B) Private blockchain — single organization
     controls all validation
- C) Consortium blockchain — multiple
     organizations share control — vetted
     participants only ✅
- D) Hybrid blockchain — combines elements
     of all three types simultaneously

> **Explanation:**
> - ✅ **C — Consortium blockchain:**
>   A **consortium blockchain** is controlled
>   by a **group of organizations** rather than
>   a single entity or the general public.
>   Participants are vetted and known —
>   typically companies in the same industry
>   (e.g., banks, shipping companies). It is
>   **semi-decentralized** — no single
>   organization dominates, but it is not
>   open to everyone. Examples: **R3 Corda**
>   (banking consortium), **Quorum** (JPMorgan),
>   **TradeLens** (Maersk + IBM — supply chain).
> - ❌ **A:** A public blockchain is fully open —
>   anyone can participate as a user, miner,
>   or validator without permission. Bitcoin
>   and Ethereum are public blockchains. The
>   question asks for the SEMI-decentralized
>   model — that is consortium.
> - ❌ **B:** A private blockchain is controlled
>   by a SINGLE organization — it is
>   centralized with restricted access.
>   Examples: Hyperledger Fabric deployed
>   by a single enterprise. The question asks
>   for MULTIPLE organizations sharing control
>   — that is consortium, not private.
> - ❌ **D:** A hybrid blockchain blends public
>   and private elements — some data is public,
>   some is private. Dragonchain is an example.
>   The question specifically describes
>   MULTIPLE organizations sharing control
>   — which is the consortium model, not hybrid.
